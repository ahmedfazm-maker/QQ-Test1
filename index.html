1.4.1

How character is assessed

Indirect questions about specific past situations, never self-description

"Are you resilient?" measures how someone would like to be seen. Nobody answers no. The same principle that governs customer interviews applies here: ask about a specific past situation and the behaviour reveals itself without the founder being asked to characterise themselves.

The trait being assessed is never named in the question. What a founder chooses to describe, what they leave out, and where they place responsibility are all part of the answer.

What is done
  • Ask about the last time, not the general case
  • Ask what they did, not what they believe
  • Ask about a failure before asking about a success
  • Follow up on what is left out — the omission is usually where the answer is
  • Require two independent signals before recording a dimension
What is not done
  • No psychometric instrument. This is not a clinical assessment.
  • No founder is scored or ranked as a person
  • No trait asserted from a single answer
  • No inference at all for a proxy-captured founder
  • Nothing hidden — each founder sees what was recorded

3.1.5

Conclusion

What the research establishes about this market

This is a small, fragmented market with a weak incumbent and a regulatory driver arriving. Thirty-four mid-size distributors can be counted by name, and they are the only group that both carries the cost of a cold-chain failure and controls the handling that causes it. That single alignment is what makes the market addressable at all — the other three segments either receive the consequence without the ability to act, or push the liability away contractually.

What they use today is not a competitor. It is a data logger, a spreadsheet and part of somebody's week, rated 4 out of 10 by the people using it, and costing EGP 40–90K a year per operator. That money is already committed, which means a replacement is a substitution decision rather than a new budget line — the single most useful fact in this section.

The opportunity sits at the custody handovers. Responsibility changes hands three to five times between manufacturer release and pharmacy shelf, and no player in the landscape records the temperature state at those moments. Global comparables show the mechanism works — EU food compliance uses handover attestation, retail loss analytics attributes loss to a node rather than a period — but both rely on something Egypt does not have, so what transfers is the mechanism and not the enforcement.

The constraint is size. A serviceable market of EGP 3.7M is a real business and a difficult venture case. Nothing in this section resolves that, and the sizing itself rests on an untested price assumption. Both are carried forward.

Combination logic

The three solutions are not three products. They are three readings of one record.

Capturing temperature at each custody transfer produces a stream of handover events. Attribution reads that stream to locate a loss. The inspection file is the same stream rendered chronologically for a regulator. Driver accountability is the same stream grouped by leg.

Build the record once and all three follow. That is why prob_04 can later be dropped without losing capability, and why the insurance solution — which needs the same record to price risk — remains reachable at a later stage.

That is why cold-chain loss shows up as an unexplained cost rather than as a priced service failure.

4.2.1

Basis of segmentation

What was used to divide the industry, and why

Segmented by role in the value stream, filtered by operational scale. Role is the primary basis because the six steps in 4.1 determine which problems an organisation experiences and whether it can act on them. Scale is the secondary filter because within the distributor role, behaviour changes materially between a 25-vehicle operator and a 190-vehicle one — different approval processes, different existing systems, different willingness to change a procedure.

Geography was considered and rejected as a basis. All four segments operate nationally, and access differs by relationship rather than by location. It is applied later as an entry filter, not as a segment.

The test that produced these four
Two organisations in the same segment should respond to the same problem in the same way. Two organisations in different segments should not. On that test, splitting distributors by fleet size is a real segmentation — the approval process changes at roughly 150 vehicles, where an IT function and a procurement process appear.

4.3.4

What the size means

Read plainly, including where it is uncomfortable

A serviceable market of EGP 7.43M is roughly USD 155,000 a year at complete penetration of the segment. Taking every one of the 34 operators, at full fleet, at this price, produces a business under EGP 7.5M of annual revenue.

That is a real business. It is a difficult venture case, and the difficulty is structural rather than a matter of execution — there are 34 buyers and no amount of selling produces a 35th.

Three routes widen it, and each is examined in the strategy work rather than here: adjacent verticals sharing the same handling problem, neighbouring geographies, and moving up the value stream into claims or insurance. Only the first requires no new capability.

4.6.1

Market summary

What the five angles establish together

This is a small, structurally coherent market with a weak incumbent, a regulatory driver arriving, and a genuine capability gap at the point where value is lost.

The industry moves pharmaceutical product through six steps, and custody changes hands at four of them. Three of those transfers sit consecutively with one party — the distributor — and none produces a temperature record. That single structural fact explains why losses are discovered as monthly totals rather than as events, and why the party who suffers them is also the party who could fix them if they had the data.

Four segments exist and they behave genuinely differently: sales cycles vary fourfold, decision-maker counts vary twofold, and only two of the four can act on the problem at all. Mid-size distributors are the segment that holds all three unmeasured steps, carries the loss, controls the handling and already spends EGP 40–90K a year on a method its own users rate 4 out of 10. That existing spend is the single most useful fact in this assessment — it means a sale is a substitution decision rather than a new budget line.

Computed per monitored vehicle, the serviceable market is EGP 7.43M a year and the obtainable market over three years is EGP 2.62M. Behaviour is favourable in most respects: the incumbent method is disliked, the market is fragmented with no player above roughly 9% share, cold-chain volume is growing, and a regulatory tightening is signalled. It is unfavourable in two that matter — buyers have never bought software of this kind, and an adjacent vendor already bills them per vehicle.

Across fourteen players in three tiers, handover-level attribution is offered by nobody locally or regionally. Two global players have built it, one of them to a EUR 620M valuation, and neither operates here or prices for this market. The gap exists because the market is small, not because the capability is difficult — which is both the opportunity and the warning.

5.1.7

Conclusion

The model, summarised

This is a recurring-revenue business selling attribution to the one party in the value stream that both carries cold-chain loss and controls the handling that causes it.

Two segments, forty organisations between them, sharing the same three offerings. What separates them is not what they receive but why they take it — mid-size distributors buy the Handover Record for loss reduction and keep the Compliance File because leaving costs them the history; large distributors buy the Compliance File for inspection standing and take attribution as support. The same product, two different conversations, and the priority inversion is the most commercially consequential fact in this frame.

Delivery rests on one behaviour the business does not control. All three offerings trace back to a supervisor performing a scan at every custody transfer, and both secondary offerings are second readings of the record that scan produces. That concentration is what makes the model efficient — nothing is built twice — and it is also the single point at which it fails.

The unit works. EGP 490 of contribution per vehicle-month at 75.4%, with cost built from the activities rather than estimated. What the unit does not show is the cash shape: sensors are bought before subscriptions are collected, so growing faster deepens the trough before it improves it.

Strongest corner

WHO

Both segments profiled from counted organisations, observed buying behaviour and 11 validation interviews. It is the corner with the most evidence behind it, which is what a customer-oriented model requires.

Weakest corner

WHY

The Handover Record price is validated at pilot scale only and untested at full fleet. The Lane Review fee is benchmarked against consultancy rates and has never been offered to a buyer.

Binding constraint

One behaviour, at the customer's site

Every offering depends on the handover scan being performed. No amount of product work removes that dependency — it is answered by adoption evidence, not by design.

Why this order

The roadmap is built to create momentum toward profitable, sustainable growth — each phase makes the next one cheaper, faster or more certain, and no phase adds cost before the thing that cost depends on has been proven.

Phase 1 proves the product does something, not that it exists. The distinction matters because two customers have already paid for a version of this and left, and the reason one gave was that it told them what they already knew. Proving delivery before proving consequence would repeat that.

Phase 2 removes the founder from the sale before any spend is added. Hiring a commercial lead is the first real cost increase in the plan, and it is placed immediately after there is something proven to sell. Adding it earlier would spend runway learning what phase 1 establishes for free.

Phase 3 tests retention and expansion together, because they are the same question. Acquisition cost does not repay at pilot scale — only a customer who expands to full fleet returns it. A retention number without an expansion number would look healthy and prove nothing about the economics.

Phase 4 is a decision, not an activity. It has two shapes and which one applies is settled by evidence from phase 3 rather than by preference now.

5.2.5

Conclusion

The direction, summarised

The direction is to become the only cold-chain record in this market that says where a loss happened, and to get there by proving one thing at a time in an order set by dependency rather than ambition.

Four phases across three years. Each proves a single condition the next one needs: that attribution changes what a customer does, that the sale works without the founder, that customers stay and expand, and finally whether the mechanism travels beyond pharmaceutical distribution. Phase 4 is deliberately written as a decision with two shapes, because which one applies is settled by evidence from phase 3 rather than by preference today.

The sequence is bounded by an internal constraint rather than an external one. Phase 1 is five months because that is the shortest founder commitment in the team, and every phase after it is spaced by how long the thing it proves actually takes to observe — three sales cycles for phase 2, twelve months of cohort for phase 3. Neither can be compressed by working harder.

What makes this a strategy rather than a plan is the governance. Eight defined conditions re-open it, two of them mandatory, and one is written explicitly to say that being behind schedule is not one of them. Outside those conditions the strategy is executed rather than debated.

Strongest element

The phase-1 end trigger

It requires customers to act on an attribution, not merely to pay. It comes directly from the stated reason a real customer stopped paying, which makes it the best-evidenced condition in the direction.

Weakest element

Everything after month 6 assumes funding closes

Phase 2 onward is built on a raise that has not happened. If it slips, the roadmap does not shift by the same amount — phase 1's bounding factor expires first.

Binding constraint

Thirty-four addressable buyers

The market caps what the roadmap can produce. so_04 exists because of it, and it is the only objective that changes the ceiling rather than the progress toward it.

6.2.9

Conclusion

The product, summarised

The product is a scan and a record. Everything else reads that record three ways.

A sensor in each vehicle captures temperature continuously; a supervisor's scan at each custody handover segments that stream into legs. The segmentation is the entire differentiator — an excursion falls inside a known leg with a known handler at a known place — and it is why six sections, twenty-four features and six architecture layers all reduce to protecting one fifteen-second interaction performed by the person who gains least from it.

Of twenty-four features, six are built, five are bought or integrated, four are covered by AI and two are explicitly excluded. Everything built is the differentiator or something it depends on. AI does more than shorten the build: it moves recommended corrective action from a v2 feature needing an analyst per twelve customers into the MVP — the exact capability whose absence caused a paying customer to leave. That is a scope decision, not a technology one.

The MVP tests one thing, with a threshold set on 12 October before any code was written: whether scan compliance holds at 85% four weeks after anyone is watching. Attribution is done by hand in the meantime, which is legitimate and is recorded as the real cost it is.

Strongest element

The build/buy split

Nothing commodity is built. Authentication, permissions, firmware, calibration and location are all purchased, which is what makes a two-person phase 1 credible.

Weakest element

The AI quality bars are untested

"A supervisor recognises the recommendation in 8 of 10 cases" is a stated bar with no measurement behind it yet. Two customer-facing capabilities rest on it.

Binding constraint

Scan compliance

Every feature, every layer and both secondary offerings degrade with each missed scan. No product decision removes this — it is answered by the MVP or it is not answered.

Commercial architecture

How the business model becomes things a customer can buy

Single product, usage-scaled, with a discrete paid service alongside it. One subscription priced per monitored vehicle per month, and a quarterly Lane Review charged per engagement.

The logic follows the buying process rather than the category. A mid-size operator approves up to EGP 150K a year at operations-director level; a per-vehicle subscription starting at pilot scale lands far below that ceiling, which means the first purchase needs one signature rather than a board conversation. Spend then grows as the customer instruments more vehicles — expansion without a second sale, which is what makes the acquisition cost repay.

Tiering by feature was considered and rejected. With three offerings where two are readings of the same record, a feature tier would mean withholding the Compliance File — the strongest retention mechanism in the model — in order to sell it back later.

Entry point

Paid pilot · 10 vehicles, 1 site

EGP 6,500/month for three months. Below the approval ceiling, short enough not to need a business case, and the format 10 of 11 operators said they expect.

Expansion path

Pilot → full fleet → additional sites

A 28-vehicle operator moving from pilot to full fleet goes from EGP 6,500 to EGP 17,390 a month. No new sale, no new approval above the same ceiling.

Never unbundled

Separating them would sell the moat as a line item to be negotiated at renewal. The file is included at no charge, permanently.

The commercial approach, summarised

One subscription priced on a unit the customer already counts, entered through a pilot small enough for one signature, and grown by instrumenting more vehicles rather than by selling again.

The price is anchored to substitution — the EGP 40–90K a year already committed to loggers and reconciliation labour, confirmed by ten of eleven operators. That is the strongest anchor available, because money already spent needs no new approval to redirect. It is not a cheaper-than argument: at full fleet Sanad costs more than the method it replaces, and it answers a question that method cannot answer at all.

The two purchases carry two different arguments and this is the most consequential thing in the section. The pilot pays back in 12.4 months against a stated tolerance of nine, so it cannot be sold on return — it is sold as a replacement that finally names the stop. The full-fleet rollout pays back in 4.8 months and is sold on return. A sales conversation that confuses the two loses on arithmetic the buyer can do in their head.

Tiering is marginal rather than flat, after a cliff was found at the twentieth vehicle where an expanding customer would have paid less in total than before. Correcting it moved the obtainable market from EGP 2.26M to EGP 2.50M and removed a term that could not have been defended at a renewal.

The substitution anchor

Ten of eleven confirmed the incumbent spend band. It is the best-evidenced input in the tactical stage and it removes the longest step in the sale.

Weakest element

Full-fleet price is untested

The growth direction, summarised

Thirty-four buyers, every one of whom can be named, reached by a founder walking into their depot. Growth here is a sales motion with a marketing layer that exists only to make the approach warm, and almost every conventional tactic is deliberately absent.

The funnel is unusual in two ways that change everything downstream. Six tactics are recorded as not used, each with its reason. Search, paid advertising, influencer, inbound, loyalty and product-led are all spend without a target in a market this size — and writing down why prevents the debate recurring quarterly.

The diagnostic walkthrough

Weakest element

Every conversion rate is a target

Binding constraint

One person can only visit so many depots

A founder-fronted motion with an 8–15 week cycle and a physical survey caps growth at roughly four walkthroughs a month until so_02 is met. No marketing tactic relieves it.

Smart operations principles

Every task below is designed against these eight

Design for the promise

Every standard traces to a brand promise or experience attribute. Here: "no excursion reported without its location" is an operational standard, not a marketing line.

Machine by default, human by decision

Automation is the starting assumption. Test: why does a person do this? "We always have" is not an answer.

Human where judgement, relationship or consequence lives

Here: the inspection file review, the Lane Review, and any conversation about a written-off consignment.

One owner per task

Accountability does not distribute. Where the owner is a founder in the evening, that is written down rather than implied.

Instrument what you run

Every task has a KPI. A task with no measure cannot be improved, delegated or fairly assessed.

Written before repeated

A task done twice gets an SOP. Test: could a new joiner do this from the description alone?

Designed for actual conditions

34 °C loading bay, 6am, driver waiting, no signal. Test: would this survive there?

Efficient enough, effective first

A faster task producing the wrong outcome is not an improvement. Effectiveness is assessed before efficiency, always.

Conclusion

The operation, summarised

Twenty-three tasks across nine functions, of which three carry most of the load and all three are manual.

Nightly attribution, the site survey and the Lane Review together account for the majority of human hours, which is why the machine share reads 48% by task count and 31% by load. The load figure is the true one, and reporting the other would make a founder-dependent operation look like an automated one.

AI does real work here, but in a specific shape: it triages support, drafts recommendations and reviews, assembles inspection files, and detects scan decay. In every case it drafts or flags and a person decides. The one place that discipline is absolute is the inspection file, where a wrong output is a regulatory liability sitting with the customer's QA manager under her signature — it is AI-assembled and human-reviewed before every single export, and that review time stays in the load rather than being optimised away.

What breaks first is knowable and dated. At nine customers, nightly attribution is 6.75 hours a day and the operation stops. That is not a risk to monitor; it is the reason automated attribution sits in phase 2.

Strongest element

The gap declaration standard

The brand promise turned into a same-day SLA, at precisely the point where an operational shortcut would break it. Most promises never survive contact with operations; this one is written as one.

Weakest element

The 1:400 support ratio

It assumes AI triage keeps support sub-linear at roughly 4 contacts per customer per week. Neither the contact rate nor the triage accuracy has been measured, and the whole cost model leans on it.

Binding constraint

The site survey lives in one head

Six hours per customer of judgement built on eleven years in the sector. It cannot be automated, it has not been documented, and no customer can be onboarded without it.

9.7.3

What to watch, specific to Sanad

Six things that would cost these founders more than they expect

Drag-along, with an unresolved exit disagreement

One founder wants an acquisition; one wants to keep building. A drag-along resolves that disagreement in favour of whoever holds the majority — and after a full-ask round, neither founder does. Settle the exit question before signing anything with a drag-along in it, not after.

Option pool created pre-money

A 12% pool taken pre-money costs the founders roughly 12 points before the round prices. It is the term founders most often fail to notice and it is worth more than most of the points they do negotiate.

Founder vesting with no credit for time served

Vesting will be required and is reasonable. What is negotiable is credit for the period already worked — two years of building on a prototype that exists. Ask for it.

Investor share above a third

The full EGP 7.44M ask produces 45.3% at the midpoint valuation. That is not a term to negotiate — it is a signal that the raise and the valuation do not fit, and staging is the answer.

Liquidation preference above 1×

At a moderate exit — which is the likely outcome given the ceiling — a 1.5× or participating preference takes a disproportionate share of the founders' proceeds. It matters more here than in a business with a large upside.

Full ratchet anti-dilution

Severe in a down round, and a down round is a real possibility given how much rests on one untested assumption. Broad-based weighted average is the standard and should be held.
