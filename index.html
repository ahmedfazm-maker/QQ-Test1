Business profile

What we understood your business to be, reconstructed from your documents rather than your deck. Worth checking we got it right.
Meshwar sells a fleet dispatch and proof-of-delivery platform to FMCG distributors in Greater Cairo and Alexandria. Revenue is a per-vehicle monthly subscription (EGP 340–520) plus a one-off onboarding fee, billed quarterly in advance.
Nineteen paying distributors across 1,140 tracked vehicles. Recognised revenue of EGP 5.9M in FY24, growing 31% year on year. Eleven staff on payroll, two further contributors invoicing as contractors. Incorporated in Egypt as a single LLC.

The business you're selling and the business you're pitching aren't the same one — and the one you're selling looks like the stronger story.

The three marked "unidentified"
These are risks we found that hadn't been raised in your materials or your answers. That distinction matters more than it might seem: an investor reads an identified risk as a business problem, and an unidentified one as information about the founder. It's the difference between "they know and they're working on it" and "they don't know their own business."
All three are cheap to move. Naming the key-person risk and writing down what would happen if you were unavailable takes an afternoon. The contractor assignments take a week. Cohort tracking takes a sprint. Doing all three moves you from Partial to Substantial without changing anything about the business itself.
What this means for raising
Your unmeasured areas are not neutral — an investor counts them as risk. Not knowing your churn isn't the same as having good churn, and your confidence number of 0.71 will be read as "29% of this business is still unknown" rather than as a data-quality note.
On the current profile, the honest read is that venture capital may not be the right instrument for this business as it stands. That isn't a criticism — a business growing 31% organically with real switching costs is one many investors would like to own. It's a question of which kind. Revenue-based financing or venture debt would suit your profile better today, and either would cost you no equity.
If equity is the goal, the work is on the potential axis first — expansion evidence, margin structure at scale — and only then on materials. Fixing the risk side alone makes you a safer version of the same-sized outcome.

In short

If you read nothing else in this report.
You've built something people pay for, in a market you understand better than most people who'll ever compete in it — and you did it without a marketing function. That's the hard part, and it's done.
What's not done is the layer that turns it into a company someone can invest in. Your margin is 16 points lower than you think, your runway is 7 months rather than 18, and after twenty months of trading you still can't answer whether your customers stay. None of those is fatal, and none of them is about the product.
The one thing holding you back: you can't measure your own business. Retention, real margin, actual spending — all of it is either untracked or tracked wrongly, which is why three of your four weakest areas trace back to the same root.
What happens next: fix measurement first, then the margin structure, then the story. On that path, we'd expect you to clear the threshold for investor materials in roughly a quarter. Raising before then means raising on numbers you can't defend.
